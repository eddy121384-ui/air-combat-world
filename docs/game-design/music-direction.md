# Skyfront: Homeland — Music Direction

Status: Working design direction  
Scope: Core soundtrack identity for real-world city content

## 1. Music Thesis

Skyfront music is not background filler. It is part of the player fantasy.

The soundtrack should amplify the feeling of:

> **Fly the aircraft you love. Fight for the skies you know.**

The emotional center is not conquest or generic military triumph. It is **place, belonging, duty, memory, and the person inside the cockpit**.

For each featured city, the core soundtrack should be built around three complementary songs:

1. **City Song** — what this place feels like.
2. **Air Force Song** — where this air force came from and what it believes it is carrying forward.
3. **Pilot Song** — who the person in the cockpit is and why they still choose to fly.

This gives every city a three-layer musical identity:

> **Place → Institution → Person**

or, from the player's point of view:

> **Where am I? → What tradition am I part of? → Why am I here?**

These songs should not feel like three interchangeable battle tracks. They should be three different perspectives on the same sky.

---

## 2. City Song

### Purpose

The City Song represents the city itself: its mood, rhythm, memory, nightlife, weather, loneliness, confidence, contradictions, and sense of home.

It does **not** need to describe military action. In many cases it should work as a standalone contemporary song outside the game.

### Design principle

Avoid postcard nationalism and stereotyped ethnic instrumentation.

A city should not be made culturally recognizable merely by adding obvious traditional instruments. Identity should come from language, phrasing, production choices, lyrical imagery, atmosphere, and local attitude.

Examples of possible city identities:

- **Taipei** — rain, humid night air, familiar lights, overpasses, rivers, returning home, irreverence, warmth beneath metropolitan cool.
- **Tokyo** — speed, precision, solitude inside a huge city, artificial light, emotional distance.
- **Shanghai** — vertical ambition, growth, momentum, river, glamour, acceleration, disorientation.
- **New York** — refusal to stop, density, confidence, survival, sleepless movement.

### Language as identity

Local language can be a primary cultural signal.

For Taipei, a strong direction is a modern Taiwanese-language track using **Taiwanese Hokkien in contemporary pop production**, rather than treating Taiwanese as something that must sound traditional.

The production can draw from sleek dance-pop, electro-pop, contemporary girl-group production, rock, electronic bass, and cinematic air-combat energy while the lyrics remain distinctly Taiwanese.

Working Taipei hook:

> **好膽袂走。**

This line should function as a signature hook rather than exposition. It can mean both a direct dogfight taunt — "if you've got the nerve, don't run" — and a wider attitude of refusing to back down.

A possible arrangement concept is to strip the instrumental before the hook, deliver **「好膽袂走」** close and dry, then let the beat, guitars, synths, and engine energy slam back in.

The goal is not to create a "traditional Taiwanese song." The goal is to make Taiwanese sound completely at home inside a modern metropolitan pop track.

## 3. Air Force Song

### Purpose

The Air Force Song represents the historical memory, traditions, institutional character, and inherited sense of duty of the air force associated with that city or country.

It must **not** be generic military hype.

It must also avoid defaulting to Hollywood-style individual hero worship, conquest fantasy, or "we are the strongest" triumphalism.

### Core principle

> **Do not sing “I am the hero.” Sing “Why are we still here?”**

The song should express continuity across generations: people, aircraft, bases, missions, losses, procedures, traditions, and the fact that someone still takes the next alert and climbs into the next cockpit.

Possible emotional material includes:

- readiness and alert duty;
- discipline and procedure;
- inherited responsibility;
- technological change across generations;
- pilots and crews who did not return;
- rebuilding after loss;
- ground crews, radar operators, controllers, maintainers, planners, and logistics personnel;
- the relationship between individual courage and a much larger system;
- what that air force historically had to become in response to geography, politics, technology, or strategic circumstance.

### Historical reference rule

Every Air Force Song should be based on a dedicated **historical reference sheet** before lyrics are finalized.

The reference sheet should identify:

- important historical periods;
- formative events;
- recurring operational traditions;
- institutional mottos or language, where appropriate;
- distinctive mission culture;
- generational changes in aircraft or doctrine;
- losses, rebuilding, and inherited memory;
- symbols, phrases, or details that can be embedded subtly rather than explained directly.

Lyrics may quote, allude to, transform, or hide these references, but they should not become a history lecture.

The ideal result works on two levels:

- a listener with no historical knowledge hears weight, continuity, and character;
- a listener who knows the history recognizes specific references underneath the lyrics.

### Glory without triumphalism

"Glory" should mean more than victory.

It can mean:

- taking the alert when nobody will know your name;
- following procedure under pressure;
- maintaining the aircraft that allows someone else to return;
- inheriting a mission from people who are gone;
- continuing despite unfavorable circumstances;
- belonging to an institution larger than one individual.

The Air Force Song should therefore feel collective rather than centered on a single ace.

A useful emotional image is:

> The names on the flight line have changed many times. The lights still come on every morning.

The exact historical personality should differ by country. No air force should receive the same song with names and aircraft swapped.

## 4. Pilot Song

### Purpose

The Pilot Song represents the human being inside the aircraft.

This is the most personal of the three tracks. It should answer questions the City Song and Air Force Song cannot:

- What is the pilot afraid of?
- Who is waiting below?
- What parts of the city do they recognize from above?
- Why did they choose this life?
- What does home look like through a canopy?
- What happens in the seconds between duty and fear?
- What would they regret leaving unsaid?

The Pilot Song does not have to be the most explosive track. It may be intimate, restrained, romantic, lonely, angry, or exhausted.

Its job is to turn the aircraft from an icon into a place occupied by a person.

The central perspective is:

> **The city is below. The institution is behind me. I am the one sitting here now.**

---

## 5. Shared Musical DNA

The three songs for one city should be distinct but connected.

Each city should have a small recurring **musical motif** — for example, a short melodic phrase, rhythm, harmonic movement, vocal figure, or texture — that can reappear differently across all three tracks.

Example:

- City Song: motif appears as a vocal hook or synth phrase.
- Air Force Song: the same motif becomes a restrained brass/synth ostinato or larger cinematic progression.
- Pilot Song: the motif returns on piano, guitar, humming, or a sparse vocal line.

This creates subconscious city identity without forcing every song into the same genre.

## 6. Taipei Working Direction

Taipei is the first concrete test case for this framework.

### Taipei — City Song

Working concept:

> **Modern Taiwanese Hokkien female electro-pop / dance-pop with rock and cinematic air-combat energy.**

The track should feel contemporary, metropolitan, confident, and recognizably Taiwanese through language and attitude rather than traditional-instrument shorthand.

Working hook:

> **好膽袂走。**

Potential imagery:

- humid night wind;
- rain on glass;
- Xinyi lights;
- rivers and bridges;
- elevated roads;
- city lights below the aircraft;
- leaving and returning;
- challenge, flirtation, confidence, home.

### Taipei — Air Force Song

The song should be researched before lyric writing.

It should draw on the history, traditions, readiness culture, generational continuity, losses, modernization, alert duty, and institutional memory of the Republic of China Air Force without becoming a propaganda anthem or American-style lone-hero song.

The point is not "we are invincible." The point is that generations change, aircraft change, and somebody still takes the next alert.

### Taipei — Pilot Song

This song should be personal rather than institutional.

A useful perspective is a pilot flying over a city whose rivers, bridges, mountains, neighborhoods, and lights are personally recognizable. The people below may never know who is above them or what happened that night.

The emotional center should be **home seen from the cockpit**.

## 7. Production Rules

1. **No filler tracks.** Major tracks should be strong enough to survive outside the game.
2. **Gameplay handles combat; lyrics handle people.** Do not waste lyrics narrating obvious on-screen actions.
3. **No generic patriotic template.** Each country's Air Force Song must grow from its own history and institutional character.
4. **No automatic American heroism.** Individual ace mythology should not become the default emotional grammar.
5. **No postcard ethnicity.** Cultural identity should not rely on stereotyped instrumentation.
6. **Research before reference.** Historical allusions in Air Force Songs must be traceable to real sources before they are locked.
7. **Local language is allowed to be modern.** Taiwanese, Japanese, Mandarin, English, or other local languages can sit inside contemporary pop, rock, electronic, or hybrid production without being forced into traditional genres.
8. **City identity should be audible.** Even without looking at the screen, a player should eventually be able to associate the song's language, motif, emotional texture, and production identity with its city.

---

## 8. North Star

For every city, the soundtrack should eventually answer three questions:

> **City Song — What does this place feel like?**  
> **Air Force Song — What history are we carrying into the sky?**  
> **Pilot Song — Why am I the one flying tonight?**

If all three work together, Skyfront's music stops being background scoring and becomes part of the fantasy of fighting over a real place that means something to the player.
